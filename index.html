UX Writing · Cvent
Workflow Hub: Meeting Management Redesign
Lead Content Designer · A/B Testing via Maze · Figma Variables · 2024

The problem

Cvent's Meeting Management platform split its workflow components across separate, siloed pages. Admins setting up Auto Event Creation, Budget Policies, and other automations couldn't see how those pieces connected. They'd change one setting and break something else, then file a support ticket when they couldn't figure out why.

  • No visual map of component relationships or dependencies
  • Labels like "Triggers" meant nothing to users. The terminology was engineering shorthand, not user language.
  • Error states gave no guidance on what to fix or what would break downstream

What I did

I introduced a centralised Workflow Hub, consolidating all components under one interface. Renamed "Triggers" to "Workflow Automations" and wrote consistent labels and tooltips that explained each component's role in context.

I created a Workflow Map showing how settings in one area affected others. This let users make changes with confidence instead of guessing.

Content was A/B tested via Maze: two versions with different label language and step sequencing. The version with better task completion and navigation scores shipped.

Figma Variables managed content states across light/dark mode and active/disabled states. What used to take hours across dozens of screens took seconds, and dev handoff was cleaner because tokens were standardised.

Copy examples

Dashboard heading: "Your Workflow at a Glance: see and manage your event automation process."
Inline guidance: "Changes here affect notifications sent during the Event Planning phase. Check your notification preferences before saving."
Error message: "This trigger conflicts with your Notification settings. Update your Notification rules to continue."

Carina Editorial Components Framework
Lead Content Designer · 40+ components · 2024

The problem

Same component, five different writers, five different choices. One tooltip said "This helps you..." Another said "Use this to..." A third skipped the explanation entirely. No one was wrong. There just wasn't a standard.

What I built

A component-by-component style guide covering every major UI pattern: accordion, avatar, badge, button, card, checkbox, date picker, dropdown, flyout, form, icon, modal, multi-select, navigation, pagination, progress indicator, slider, table, textbox, and more.

For each component:

  • Tone: which register fits (e.g. friendly for empty states, direct for errors)
  • Character and word limits, so labels don't break layouts
  • Do's and Don'ts with concrete examples, not vague principles
  • A11Y: ARIA labels, alt text patterns, what screen readers need
  • L10N: what breaks in translation, symbols to avoid, text expansion allowances
  • CTA standards: action-verb-first, character limits, no "Click here"

Content Audit with Flesch-Kincaid Scoring
Lead Content Designer · Full + Rolling audit model · 2024

The problem

Content decisions were made by whoever spoke loudest in the room. No data, no framework, no way to prioritise. I wanted content strategy at Cvent to be defensible, backed by numbers, not preference.

The approach

Two audit modes: a full audit for a complete content inventory, and a rolling audit for ongoing quality maintenance. Standards didn't slip between reviews.

Flesch-Kincaid scoring sat at the centre. Every piece of product content was scored against the FK readability formula. Target range was 60-80 (7th-9th grade), readable for global enterprise users without being oversimplified.

The 10-dimension scoring model covered: strategy alignment, usefulness, accuracy, clarity, accessibility, readability, inclusivity, findability, engagement, and action quality.

The framework became repeatable. New content gets audited against the same standard, making quality measurable over time rather than a subjective call.

UX Writing · Cvent
Cutting Subordinating Conjunctions Across the Product
Lead Content Designer · Style guide + training + audit · 2023

The problem

Across Cvent's product, sentences regularly started with "Because," "While," "Although," and "Once." These constructions bury the action. Users scanning a notification or error message miss the thing they actually need to do.

What changed

Updated the style guide with a clear rule and restructuring alternatives. Ran workshops where writers rewrote real examples in real time. Audited existing product content and revised alongside the team.

Real examples from the product

Before
Your virtual vendor card request for GBP 400 has been submitted. Once approved, [Mastercard] will share the card details with (abc@vendor.com), via a secure email.
After
Your virtual vendor card request for GBP 400 has been submitted. You will receive your card details at abc@vendor.com upon approval.
Before
While the report is being generated, you can continue browsing other sections.
After
Generate the report while browsing other sections.
Before
Because you missed the deadline, the registration is closed.
After
The registration is closed.

Impact

  • Faster scannability. The outcome appears before the condition.
  • Shorter strings improve localization accuracy and speed
  • More consistent, direct brand voice across all surfaces

Cross-Functional Collaboration Framework
Lead Content Designer · Eng, Product, Support, Marketing · 2023

The problem

Content design was always downstream. By the time the copy request arrived, the IA was locked, the flows were built, and the only option was to write around a broken structure. I wanted to change when content designers entered the process.

What I did

Discovery: talked to engineering, product, support, and marketing to map every existing touchpoint and every gap where content design wasn't involved but should have been.

From there I built a documented collaboration model showing when and how content designers engage across the product lifecycle: ideation, design, build, ship, post-launch.

Implementation ran as structured 1-hour sessions with stakeholders. Each covered role overviews, touchpoint mapping, open Q&A, and agreed next steps.

Result

  • Earlier content involvement meant fewer structural rewrites late in the sprint
  • Teams outside UX understood what content design actually does and when to bring us in
  • The framework scaled as new teams joined without needing to be rebuilt each time

AI-Powered Style Guide and Error Framework
Lead Content Designer · All Birdeye product surfaces · 2025

The context

Birdeye's platform spans reviews, listings, surveys, social media, and messaging. Getting consistent content across all of those, written by different designers across different sprints, required a system, not a document.

What I built

  • Error message frameworks: empathetic, actionable patterns for validation errors, permission errors, network failures, and empty states, with templates for each
  • Messaging patterns: reusable templates for confirmations, onboarding nudges, tooltips, and notifications
  • Voice and tone guidance: context-sensitive rules for different surfaces and user emotional states

Meeting Space Calculator: Unit Standardisation
Lead Content Designer · Global UI · 2024

The problem

"SF," "sq ft," "ft2": three abbreviations for the same thing, in the same product, sometimes on the same page. International users had no metric option. Layout-specific space requirements were unexplained, which led to consistent calculation errors.

Research and decisions

"sq. ft." was chosen over "SF" (informal, unclear) and "ft2" (too technical). Used consistently across all UI elements, tooltips, and documentation.

Before
Total Area: 3000 SF
After
Total Area: 3,000 sq. ft. required for this setup.

A toggle was introduced for switching between sq. ft. and sq. m, with location-based defaults. One detail worth noting: the Metric system doesn't use periods in abbreviations, so "sq. m" not "sq. m." A standard Cvent wasn't following before this work.

Toggle tooltip
Click to switch between square feet (sq. ft.) and square meters (sq. m).

Layout-specific tooltips were added to explain why space requirements vary by seating type.

Tooltip
Banquet seating requires 15 sq. ft. per person. Classroom seating requires 20 sq. ft. per person due to table space.

SPP Reporting: Financial Terminology for Pharma
Lead Content Designer · Compliance reporting · 2024

The context

Pharma event managers track healthcare professional (HCP) expenses across events for regulatory compliance. The SPP (Spend Per Person) feature in Cvent's budget tool was supposed to handle this, but inconsistent labels, unclear admin setup, and no cross-event reporting made it unreliable for compliance use.

What I did

Terminology standardisation: "Spend Per Person" and "SPP" were used inconsistently across labels, descriptions, headers, and config screens. I mapped every instance and established a single consistent standard.

The setup page for SPP configuration was confusing two distinct workflows. I defined and rewrote them clearly:

Basic Configuration: Define spend per person tag to identify registrants for cost association of a budget item and to filter spend per person reports.
Tag Configuration: Create spend per person tag to identify specific registrants for cost association of budget items and filter reports.

Community guidance articles were written so pharma clients could answer their own SPP questions, reducing support load and giving compliance teams a reference they could point to.

The lesson

In regulated industries, inconsistent terminology isn't just confusing. It creates compliance risk. This project aligned with Cvent's 2024 customer-centricity goal: making the product work better for the users who depend on it most.

Cross-Team Product Workshops
Lead Content Designer · Content Design + Localization · 2023

The problem

Writers were writing for products they'd never clicked through end-to-end. Localization teams were translating strings without understanding the workflow they lived in. Quality suffered because context was missing.

The format

  • 10-15 min: Workflow overview, what it does, who uses it, what matters
  • 30 min: Hands-on walkthrough of the actual product, not slides, the real interface
  • 15 min: Open feedback, what's unclear in the flow, what's off in the copy

Sessions ran on a regular schedule, attended by both teams. No slides, no theory. Just clicking through the product together and talking through what we found.

What it changed

  • Writers made fewer assumptions about how features actually work
  • Localization teams could flag translation risks earlier, before strings were locked
  • Both teams built shared context that made collaboration faster in future sprints

Meta Case Study · 2025
Building This Portfolio Website
Lead Content Designer · Solo build · AI-assisted · 2025

Why this is a case study

Most portfolios describe work. This one is work. Building it required the same skills I use professionally: audience research, content strategy, UX decisions, technical implementation, and measurement. The difference is the client was me, which meant no stakeholder to blame when something was wrong.

Audience and strategy

The site targets three audiences: hiring managers in product-led SaaS companies, design leads evaluating senior content design candidates, and peers in the content design and UX writing community. Each has different intent. Hiring managers need to see outcomes fast. Design leads need to see process depth. Peers need to see something technically interesting enough to share.

The decision to gate some case studies was strategic, not just protective. A gated portfolio signals that the work inside is worth protecting. It also qualifies visitors. Someone who requests access is more engaged than someone who skims and bounces.

Content decisions

The headline went through several iterations. "Content that makes products click" tested too marketing-adjacent. "UX content that ships, scales, sticks" tested better with a product audience but still relied on alliteration as a crutch. The final version, "I design content. I build tools. I measure both.", uses three short declarative sentences that each add new information. It doesn't require the reader to decode a tagline.

Every case study modal was written to follow a consistent structure: problem first, then what changed, then what the outcome was. No preamble. No "in this project, I..." openers.

UX and design

The font pairing (Roboto Slab for display, Roboto for body) was chosen to signal technical credibility without looking like a developer's portfolio. Google's type system is familiar, high-contrast, and reads well at all weights.

The hero stat cards solve a common portfolio problem: visitors don't read bios, they scan numbers. Four metrics with source context replace three paragraphs of self-description. The horizontal layout anchors the stats to the hero copy rather than floating them as a separate element.

The speaking section used an auto-fill grid that left a broken partial row when the 5th card didn't complete the 4-column layout. Fixed by switching to a fixed 4-column grid and moving recognition content into a full-width contextual strip below. It gives the content more room and looks intentional rather than accidental.

Technical features

  • Terminal boot sequence: a typewriter-style animation on first load, showing real environment data (timezone, platform) to signal technical awareness. Fades out after 3 seconds.
  • Progressive blur + gate: four case studies are partially visible and require a password or email to unlock. Captures visitor intent before they access sensitive work.
  • Session fingerprinting: silently captures browser data, referrer, timezone, sections viewed, and time on site. Logs to a Google Sheet via Apps Script on exit.
  • Email capture with context: every gate request logs the email, timestamp, which case study was requested, and device type.
  • Console easter egg: a styled message waits for anyone who opens DevTools. Common behaviour among engineers and technical designers who are the most valuable audience.

AEO and discoverability

The site is structured for Answer Engine Optimisation. Each section opens with a direct, factual statement. The meta description targets the specific query a recruiter or hiring manager would run. Schema markup is the next addition: structured data for a Person entity with sameAs links to LinkedIn and conference appearances.

What I'd do differently

Build the Google Apps Script logging endpoint first, before the site went live. Right now the visitor data collection is wired but the endpoint is a placeholder. Every visitor before that's live is untracked. The measurement infrastructure should always precede the traffic, not follow it.
