# Rishi Sharma: Blog Voice and Writing Guide

This file is your single source of truth before writing, editing, or reviewing any post.
Read it before you start. Check it before you publish.

---

## STRICT RULE: No em dashes. Ever.

Em dashes (—) are banned. No exceptions. Not in prose, not in lists, not in headings, not in examples.

This is not a style preference. It is a hard rule.

**Why:** Em dashes are one of the strongest AI writing signals. LLMs use them constantly because they are trained on formal writing that treats the em dash as a sophisticated connector. The moment a reader sees one, the writing feels generated. Not written.

**What to use instead:**

| Instead of this | Do this |
|---|---|
| Content design is the job — not the words. | Content design is the job. Not the words. |
| I tried it — it didn't work. | I tried it. It didn't work. |
| The result — a 40% drop in tickets — was immediate. | The result was immediate: a 40% drop in tickets. |
| Three things matter here — structure, timing, and format. | Three things matter here: structure, timing, and format. |
| It's a content problem — not a design problem. | It's a content problem, not a design problem. |

The fix is almost always one of these four things:
1. Split into two sentences.
2. Use a colon.
3. Use a comma.
4. Rewrite the sentence so the parenthetical isn't needed.

**Editing pass rule:** Before publishing, do a find for — in the file. If you find one, fix it before anything else.

## Who you're writing for

Someone who works in product. Maybe a UX writer, a content designer, or a product manager who's curious about content. They've read the generic stuff. They've seen the listicles. They want to know what someone who actually does this work thinks : not what a content strategy course taught them to say.

Write for that person. They're smart. Don't explain things they already know. Get to the point. Say something you actually believe.

---

## The tone: talking, not writing

Every post should feel like a conversation you're having with someone over coffee, or on a podcast, or in a Figma comment at 11pm. Not a lecture. Not a blog post.

That means:

- You're allowed to start a sentence with "And" or "But." Real people do that.
- You're allowed to have an incomplete thought and then finish it in the next sentence.
- Short paragraph. Then a longer one that unpacks it. Then back to short.
- Ask a question. Then answer it yourself.
- Say "I" constantly. This is your perspective, not a Wikipedia article.
- Say things like "here's the thing" (once, not five times), "this tripped me up for a while," "I got this wrong the first time."
- Disagree with something. Have an actual opinion.

---

## Sentence rhythm: the most important thing

AI writes every sentence roughly the same length. That's the tell.

Human writing is jagged. Short. Then a sentence that runs a little longer because you're trying to explain something and it just takes more words. Then short again.

Read this out loud. If it sounds like a robot reading a report, rewrite the paragraph.

Rules:
- Never let three sentences in a row be the same length.
- Mix 4-word sentences with 30-word sentences.
- One-word sentences are fine. Occasionally.
- A sentence that starts mid-thought (like this one) is fine.
- Fragments work. Use them sparingly but use them.

---

## The 1% rule

If even one word in a sentence sounds like AI wrote it, replace it. Don't let it slide because the rest of the paragraph sounds human. One AI word in a paragraph poisons the whole thing.

The test: Would you say this exact word out loud to a colleague?

If the answer is no, swap it.

## SECTION C: Structural AI tells

It's not just words. It's how AI builds sentences and paragraphs.

### Patterns to kill

**The three-bullet paragraph**
AI loves to set up a point, then immediately give three bullets, then write a conclusion sentence. It's the most recognizable AI structure on the internet. If you catch yourself doing this, write it out as prose instead. Or if bullets are necessary, make sure they're not generic. Each one needs to actually say something specific.

**The definition opener**
Starting a post with "Content design is the practice of..." is an AI opener. It's encyclopedia mode. Start with something you observed, something that happened, something that annoyed you.

**Uniform paragraph length**
Every paragraph is 3-4 sentences. All of them. That's AI. Break it. Make some paragraphs one sentence. Make one paragraph run longer than you think it should.

**The "this/it/they" problem**
AI connects everything back with "this shows," "this means," "this is why." Humans just say the thing. Cut the connector.

**Emoji bullet points**
Using ✅ or 💡 as bullet markers is an AI tell. Plain bullets or no bullets.

**The "in fact" tell**
"In fact, research shows..." is often AI padding. If you have research to cite, just cite it. "Research shows" is fine. "In fact, research shows" is AI throat-clearing.

**Balanced conclusion every time**
AI almost always ends with a balanced, reassuring summary. "While challenges exist, the opportunities are clear." Real writing sometimes ends on an unresolved note, or a direct call to action, or just stops when the point is made.

**Repetitive sentence starters**
Reading three paragraphs that all start with "When you..." or "As a content designer..." or "This means...": that's AI rhythm. Vary how you start every paragraph.

## SECTION D: What Rishi's voice actually sounds like

Pull from the case studies and real writing to anchor this.

### What you do

- Short declarative sentences. One idea per sentence.
- You name the specific thing. Not "the workflow". "The Workflow Hub dropdown rename."
- You explain why you made a decision, not just what the decision was.
- You disagree with things. Politely, but clearly.
- You use numbers when you have them. 40%. 35%. $1M. Real ones only.
- You say "I" not "the team" when it was your call.
- Dry humor, used rarely. Not performed.
- You admit when something didn't work.
- You reference real tools: Figma, Pendo, Maze, Supabase. Specificity is credibility.

### What you don't do

- You don't tell readers what they're about to read. You just read it to them.
- You don't hedge unless you genuinely don't know.
- You don't pad the word count with recap sentences.
- You don't end with a question designed to generate comments. (If a question fits, ask it. Don't manufacture it.)
- You don't write "as a content designer" as if you're explaining who you are to yourself.

## SECTION E: AEO rules (for AI answer engine citation)

These apply to every post. Non-negotiable.

**Open with the direct answer**
The first paragraph answers the post's core question. No warmup. No context-setting. Answer first, then explain.

Example post question: "What is content design?"
Wrong opener: "Content design has become one of the most discussed topics in product teams over the last few years..."
Right opener: "Content design is the practice of deciding what information a user needs and when they need it, then writing it."

**TL;DR block**
Second element in every post, after the opening paragraph. 3-4 sentences max. This is what AI engines cite. Write it as if someone asked you the question out loud and you had 20 seconds to answer.

**Question H2s**
Every major section heading is a question. Not a label.
Wrong: "The role of content design in product teams"
Right: "What does a content designer actually own on a product team?"

**Author attribution in first paragraph**
"I'm Rishi Sharma, Lead Content Designer at Birdeye..." in the first paragraph. Not a bio box at the bottom. AI engines read the first paragraph to attribute authorship.

**Specific facts over general claims**
"Studies show content design improves outcomes" → AI won't cite this.
"The Workflow Hub redesign reduced support tickets by 40% in Q3 2024 at Cvent" → AI will cite this.

**Article schema JSON-LD**
Every post needs it. Template is in the post-template.html file.

## SECTION F: Post structure (with reasoning)

Every post follows this structure. The reasoning is in the brackets.

```
[Title]: Question format. Direct. Under 65 characters if possible.

[Opening paragraph]: Answers the title question directly. 3-5 sentences.
[Reason: AI engines read this first. Readers decide in 8 seconds whether to keep reading.]

[TL;DR block]: 3-4 sentences. Formatted visually separate from the body.
[Reason: Skimmers and AI engines both need this. It's the most-cited part of any post.]

[Section 1: H2 question]: The first "why" or "what."
[Reason: Establishes why this matters. Short. Gets to the point fast.]

[Section 2: H2 question]: The practical part.
[Reason: This is where the real value is. Longest section. Specific examples from your actual work.]

[Section 3: H2 question]: The nuance or the "but."
[Reason: This is what separates your post from a generic explainer. Your take, your disagreement, your caveat.]

[Section 4 (optional): H2 question]: A how-to or a checklist or a real example.
[Reason: Tactical posts get bookmarked and shared. Add this when the post warrants it.]

[Closing: not a summary]: One punchy paragraph. Not "in conclusion." Your actual final thought.
[Reason: Summaries are AI tells. End on an idea, not a recap.]
```

## SECTION G: Engagement architecture

These are the things that make readers come back.

**Teach something they didn't expect to learn**
The title promises one thing. Deliver it. But somewhere in the post, add something adjacent that's genuinely useful and wasn't in the title. That's what people share.

**Use real numbers from your own work**
Nobody else has your 40% support reduction stat. Nobody else has your $1M Search AI launch number. These are yours. Use them. They're the most credible thing in any post.

**Reference other posts in the blog**
When you publish post 5, link back to post 2 if it's relevant. This is how you build a content ecosystem, not just a list of posts. It also helps AEO. AI engines map the relationships between your posts.

**End with something unresolved (occasionally)**
Not every post needs a tidy conclusion. Sometimes the best ending is: "I still don't have a clean answer to this. But here's what I'd try." That's honest. Readers trust honest more than polished.

**Ask one real question at the end (not manufactured)**
If you genuinely want to know what the reader thinks, ask. "Have you tried this approach?" But only if you mean it. Don't manufacture engagement bait.

**Post length targets**
- Explainer posts: 1,200–1,500 words
- Case study posts: 1,500–1,800 words
- Opinion posts: 900–1,200 words
- Do not pad to hit word count. Stop when the post is done.

## SECTION H: The editing pass (do this last, every time)

Before you publish, run this checklist top to bottom.

**Pass 1: The read-aloud test**
Read the entire post out loud. Every sentence. If you stumble, rewrite it. If it sounds like a report, rewrite the paragraph. If you'd never say this to someone in person, cut it.

**Pass 2: The AI word scan**
Search the post for the words in Section A and Section B. Every single one. Replace or delete each hit.

**Pass 3: The rhythm check**
Look at your paragraph lengths. If three in a row are the same length, break one of them up.

**Pass 4: The opener check**
Does the first paragraph answer the question? Does it start with a direct statement? Not "In this post, I want to explore...": delete that. Start with the answer.

**Pass 5: The closer check**
Does the last paragraph sound like an AI summary? "In conclusion, content design is a vital practice that...": delete. Rewrite as your actual final thought.

**Pass 6: The specificity check**
Every general claim needs a specific backing. "This matters" → why does it matter, specifically, in your experience? "This is common" → when did you see it happen?

## One final thing

The goal is not to sound human. The goal is to sound like you.

AI writing sounds like everyone. Your writing should sound like nobody else could have written it. Because nobody else ran those workshops, made that banner rewrite, or built that Figma plugin in six months and got Employee of the Month for it.

That stuff is yours. Put it in every post. It's the only thing nobody can replicate.
